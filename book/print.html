Peterson's reading in one paragraph

Peterson reads the sermon line by line as practical psychology, not as a list of doctrines. It begins on a mountain because the first rule is to aim up, at the highest good you can conceive. From there: admit what you do not know, tell the truth, and take on burdens voluntarily, because that is what builds you. Judgment moves from outcome to intent, so anger and lust count, and reconciliation comes before worship. Do good, pray and fast in secret rather than for status. Put one aim at the top and stop worrying. Clear your own eye before judging anyone else, ask for what you want, and treat others as you want to be treated, going first. Faith is shown in what you do, not what you say, and the house built on rock is the character that has already faced catastrophe before it arrives.

Cheat Sheet

The whole lecture series on one page. Quotes are Peterson's; everything else is paraphrase.

The core idea

Peterson reads the Sermon on the Mount as practical guidance for living. Aim at the highest good you can conceive, admit what you do not know, tell the truth, and take on your burdens voluntarily. Fix your own faults before judging others, treat people the way you want to be treated, and prepare for catastrophe before it arrives, so that your life is built on rock.

Seven main points

1. Aim up. Setting your sights on the highest good orients your whole life. (The Setting, 5:1)

"And so, aim up. That's the first rule."

2. Admit what you do not know. Stability comes from competence, and competence starts with facing your ignorance. (The Beatitudes, 5:3)

"In order to move out into the world and to become competent, you have to make friends with what you don't know."

3. Tell the truth. Truthful speech is how you get the adventure that makes life worth it. (The Beatitudes, 5:6)

"What redeems your life is the adventure of your life. It's not happiness, it's not ease or security"

4. Take on burdens voluntarily. The same challenge works differently when you choose it. (The Lord's Prayer, 6:10)

"The people you admire are definitely the people who take their burdens on voluntarily."

5. Clear your own eye first. Deal with your own blindness before correcting others. (Judging, 7:3–5)

"you actually can't act as a moral social agent until you've cleaned the debris out of your own vision."

6. Treat others as you want to be treated, and go first. (The Golden Rule, 7:12)

"The price you pay is that you have to do that to other people, first."

7. Build on the rock. Facing the worst ahead of time builds a character that can withstand it. (The Wise and Foolish Builders, 7:24–27)

"It's exposure. You can walk through all of that before it happens. And then when it happens, you're ready."

One-minute version

The sermon starts on a mountain because you should aim up. Aim at the highest good and that aim fills you with something like enthusiasm, which in Greek means being filled with God. Admit what you do not know, speak the truth, and pick up your burdens on purpose. Judge your own intent, not just your outcomes, and make peace with your brother before anything else. Do good in secret, put one aim at the top, and stop worrying. Look at your own faults before other people's, ask for what you want, and treat others how you want to be treated, starting first. Act out what you believe rather than just saying it. Face catastrophe before it comes and you are the house on the rock: the storm still comes, but you do not fall.

Five quick questions

  1. If not happiness, what redeems a life? "What redeems your life is the adventure of your life." (The Beatitudes)
  2. Is forgiveness blanket absolution? "It isn't blanket absolution; it's reciprocity." (The Lord's Prayer)
  3. Does "judge not" mean never judge? "So it doesn't exactly say don't judge." (Judging)
  4. What is the best disguise of a predator? "the most effective guise of a predator is compassion." (False Prophets)
  5. When disaster hits, what is the real question? "Is it because of the tragedy or is it because you weren't ready to withstand it?" (The Wise and Foolish Builders)

The lecture

  • Speaker: Jordan B. Peterson, lecture series on the Sermon on the Mount, delivered over four days (Day 1: Matthew 5:1–16; Day 2: 5:17–48; Day 3: Matthew 6; Day 4: Matthew 7). Peterson reads from the King James Version.
  • Transcript: a private transcript of about 80,845 words, kept in a local study wiki (~/wikis/sermon-on-the-mount/, file source/sermon_full.txt). That wiki splits the transcript into 50 contiguous sections in lecture order, each with a summary, key ideas, exact quotes with word positions, and questions. Its coverage audit confirms the 50 sections cover every word with no gaps or overlaps.
  • How this wiki was made from it: the 50 sections were merged into 27 passage pages that follow the sermon's verse order. Four sections (19, 20, 31, 34) cross a passage boundary and are split between two pages. The "Peterson's reading" text on each page is a new plain-language summary; key ideas and questions come from the section notes; quotes are copied exactly from the transcript and limited to short excerpts. Word positions ("about word N") count words from the start of the transcript and are approximate.
  • Gaps: five verses are never read aloud or discussed on their own: 5:9, 5:12, 5:45, 7:8 and 7:18. See the verse map.

Caveats

The commentary is a reader's summary of a lecture, not Peterson's own words except inside quotation marks. It reflects his interpretation of the text, which is psychological rather than doctrinal. For his full argument, go to the lectures themselves.

Peterson's reading in plain words

Before he reads a single blessing, Peterson asks why the sermon is given on a mountain. His answer is that height is the oldest symbol of seeing far and aiming high. From the top you can see where you are going, so the first rule is simple: aim up.

He argues that most good feeling in life comes from seeing yourself make progress toward a worthwhile goal, not from reaching it. Without an aim, and an ethical one, you cannot feel much that is positive at all. Since you are betting your life on what you do anyway, you might as well bet it on the highest good you can imagine. He links this to taking on heavy responsibility on purpose, which he says is the real source of self-respect, and to taking on challenges voluntarily, which builds you up rather than turning you into something that hides.

  • Enlightenment happens on a mountain, not in a valley, because from above you can see; the eye of Horus, the falcon and Zazu in The Lion King carry the same symbol.
  • The word that is true and oriented by love brings habitable order out of chaos, and people participate in that with their own speech.
  • Most positive emotion comes from seeing progress toward a goal, not from attaining it; without an ethical aim you cannot experience positive emotion.
  • God classically defined as the summum bonum, the sum of all good things, is a definition, not a statement of belief.

  • Bearing a heavy responsibility is the only real answer to the burden of your own flaws, and service to others is one of the deepest pleasures of life.
  • Voluntary challenge turns on new genes and builds new parts of you; fleeing a challenge turns you into a prey animal.
  • Betrayal, death and old age are coming, so you had better be set up for the challenge.

Verses the lecture does not read aloud

Peterson reads the sermon almost verse by verse. Checking each KJV verse against the transcript, these five are never read out or discussed on their own:

  • Matthew 5:9, "Blessed are the peacemakers." The reading jumps from 5:8 to 5:10. Peacemaking comes up later, under Love Your Enemies.
  • Matthew 5:12, "Rejoice, and be exceeding glad... for so persecuted they the prophets." The idea is covered in the discussion of 5:10–11.
  • Matthew 5:45, "He maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good." Peterson reads 5:44 and 5:46–48 around it.
  • Matthew 7:8, "For every one that asketh receiveth." Covered by the discussion of 7:7.
  • Matthew 7:18, "A good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit." Covered by the discussion of 7:17 and 7:19–20.

Smaller skips: 5:1–2 is treated as the setting, and the first half of 6:32 ("for after all these things do the Gentiles seek") is passed over. Every other verse is read aloud and commented on.
