## Peterson's reading in plain words

Peterson starts from a plain wish: it would be better not to have enemies, and wishing them suffering makes nothing better. Loving your enemies means wanting them not to be enemies, of you, of themselves, or of life, and trying to bring them into the fold.

That is not naive. Between naive trust, which gets burned, and cynicism, which makes cooperation impossible, he describes courageous trust: extending goodwill while knowing people can be dangerous. Peacemaking takes fine judgment, as anyone who sets limits for a three-year-old knows. "Be ye perfect" he reads as "aim right": you may miss, but you can aim at the highest target, and it is facing the worst that calls out the best in you.

## Key ideas

- Wishing enemies hotter in hell does not make anything better.
- Love enemies by wanting them not to be enemies, of you, of themselves, or of being.
- Peacemaking means making very fine discriminations, as with a three-year-old's boundaries.
- Naive trust gets burned, cynicism makes trade impossible; courageous trust extends goodwill knowing everyone is full of snakes.
- With a paranoid client with four restraining orders, nothing beat honesty; you cannot fool a career psychopath.
- Be perfect: maybe you cannot hit the target, but you can aim right.
- The shaman confers with ancestors; rescue your dead father from the belly of the beast.
- It's only the worst that calls for the best.
